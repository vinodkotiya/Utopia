The Rider-Waite Tarot Deck: A Beginner's Guide to the Classic Cards

By Utopia Wellness & Gifts · July 5, 2026

The Rider-Waite tarot deck is the most widely recognized and recommended tarot deck in the world — and for good reason. First published in 1909, it set the standard for how tarot cards are illustrated and interpreted, making it the ideal starting point for anyone curious about tarot. Whether you're drawn to tarot for personal reflection, spiritual guidance, or creative inspiration, this deck offers an accessible gateway into a rich tradition that spans centuries.

The Rider-Waite deck is one we consistently recommend to newcomers and seasoned readers alike because its imagery speaks a universal language of symbol, colour, and story. In this guide, we'll walk you through the deck's fascinating history, its structure, what makes it unique, and how to begin reading with confidence.

The History of the Rider-Waite-Smith Deck

The deck we know today as the Rider-Waite (or more accurately, the Rider-Waite-Smith) tarot was born from a collaboration between two remarkable individuals. Arthur Edward Waite, a scholarly mystic and member of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, conceived the deck's symbolic system. He commissioned artist Pamela Colman Smith — known affectionately as "Pixie" — to bring his vision to life through illustration. The deck was first published in December 1909 by the Rider Company in London, which is where the "Rider" in the name comes from.

What made this deck revolutionary was Pamela Colman Smith's decision to create fully illustrated scenes for every card, including the Minor Arcana. Before the Rider-Waite-Smith deck, most tarot decks showed only abstract arrangements of suit symbols on the numbered cards — much like a standard playing card deck. Smith's evocative illustrations gave each card a narrative quality, making intuitive reading possible for the first time without memorising long lists of meanings.

Why It Remains the Gold Standard for Beginners and Professionals

Over a century after its creation, the Rider-Waite deck remains the most popular tarot deck worldwide. Most tarot books, courses, and online resources use it as their reference deck. When you learn with the Rider-Waite, you're building a foundation that translates easily to hundreds of other modern decks that draw on its imagery and symbolism.

  • Universal learning resource — nearly every tarot guidebook references this deck's imagery
  • Intuitive storytelling — the illustrated scenes allow you to "read" the cards even without memorising definitions
  • Rich symbolic layers — colours, numbers, flowers, animals, and astrological symbols reward deeper study over time
  • Community and support — the largest community of tarot readers uses this system, making it easy to find study groups and mentors

The Structure: 78 Cards, Infinite Possibilities

The Rider-Waite deck contains 78 cards divided into two main groups: the Major Arcana and the Minor Arcana. Together, they form a complete symbolic map of the human experience — from life's grand turning points to everyday situations and emotions.

The Major Arcana (22 Cards)

These are the deck's "big picture" cards, numbered 0 through 21. They include iconic archetypes like The Fool, The High Priestess, The Tower, and The World. Major Arcana cards represent significant life themes, spiritual lessons, and pivotal moments of transformation. When they appear in a reading, they signal that something meaningful is at play.

The Minor Arcana (56 Cards)

The Minor Arcana is divided into four suits — Wands (fire, passion, action), Cups (water, emotions, relationships), Swords (air, intellect, challenges), and Pentacles (earth, material world, health). Each suit contains cards numbered Ace through 10, plus four Court Cards: Page, Knight, Queen, and King. These cards reflect the day-to-day situations, feelings, and choices that shape our lives.

Key Features That Make the Rider-Waite Deck Unique

  • Fully illustrated pip cards — every card tells a visual story, from the joyful celebration on the Three of Cups to the quiet exhaustion on the Ten of Wands
  • Layered symbolism — Kabbalistic, astrological, and numerological references are woven throughout for readers who want to explore deeper esoteric traditions
  • Accessible colour palette — bold primary colours and clear compositions make the imagery easy to read at a glance
  • Consistent visual language — water always represents emotion, mountains represent challenges, and sunlight represents clarity, creating a reliable symbolic vocabulary

How to Start Reading with the Rider-Waite Deck

Starting a tarot practice doesn't require years of study. The beauty of the Rider-Waite deck is that you can begin immediately by simply observing the images and trusting your intuition. Here's a gentle way to begin:

  • Start with a daily card pull — each morning, shuffle your deck and draw a single card. Spend a few moments looking at the image before checking any guidebook.
  • Notice what stands out — which colours, figures, or symbols catch your eye? What feelings does the card evoke? Your first impressions are often the most insightful.
  • Keep a tarot journal — write down your daily card, your initial impressions, and any events that felt connected. Over time, you'll develop a personal relationship with the cards.
  • Learn simple spreads — once you're comfortable with single cards, try a three-card spread (past, present, future) or a five-card spread for more nuance.
  • Be patient with yourself — reading tarot is a skill that deepens with practice. There's no wrong way to interpret a card as long as you stay open and curious.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the Rider-Waite deck good for beginners?

Yes — it's widely considered the best tarot deck for beginners. The fully illustrated scenes make it possible to interpret cards intuitively without memorising meanings, and nearly all beginner tarot books use this deck as their reference.

What is the difference between the Rider-Waite and Rider-Waite-Smith deck?

They are the same deck. The name "Rider-Waite-Smith" gives proper credit to Pamela Colman Smith, the artist who illustrated all 78 cards. Many in the tarot community now prefer this fuller name to honour her essential contribution.

How many cards are in the Rider-Waite tarot deck?

The deck contains 78 cards: 22 Major Arcana cards representing life's major themes and spiritual lessons, and 56 Minor Arcana cards divided into four suits (Wands, Cups, Swords, and Pentacles) that reflect everyday experiences.

Do I need to be psychic to read tarot?

Not at all. Tarot reading is a skill that anyone can develop. The Rider-Waite deck's visual storytelling makes it especially approachable — you can begin by simply describing what you see in each card and noticing how it relates to your question or situation. Intuition grows naturally with practice.
